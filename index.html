Do graduate students gain from an AI writing tool? A pilot study

A three-semester mixed-methods evaluation of Grammarly with graduate students at the University of Alaska Fairbanks Writing Center. Presented as a poster at the Teaching and Learning with AI 2026 conference.

RoleCo-investigator; sole lead on the quantitative analysis, plus part of the qualitative analysis
TeamTwo researchers, UAF Writing Center
MethodsLongitudinal surveys, usage data, thematic analysis, non-parametric statistics
Participants18 graduate students (13 with complete data)

The problem

Graduate students are turning to AI writing tools, and universities are drafting policies about them. Student experiences are still underrepresented in that conversation. Our question: is there enough evidence to recommend Grammarly for broader adoption among graduate students?

Approach

We surveyed the same students at three points: summer 2025, fall 2025, and spring 2026. Surveys combined closed and open-ended questions with usage data from the Grammarly dashboard. Of the 18 participants who completed at least two surveys, 13 completed all three, and the statistical tests use those 13. Participants were 72% PhD and 28% Master's students, and one third were non-native English speakers.

Summer 2025

Baseline survey of writing confidence.

Fall 2025

No significant change in confidence from baseline.

Spring 2026

Significant confidence gain from the previous semester.

What we found

Confidence rose over time. A Friedman test showed a significant change across the three surveys (χ²(2) = 12.634, p = .002). Pairwise Wilcoxon tests with a Bonferroni correction located the gain between the second and third surveys.

ComparisonVpSignificant at p < .017?
Baseline to midpoint15.188No
Midpoint to end8.016Yes
Baseline to end9.5.021No (significant before correction)

Frequency of use did not explain the gains. Usage frequency was not significantly related to confidence change (rs = .302, p = .316) or to productivity (rs = .062, p = .833). Sustained engagement over time looked like the driver, not how often students opened the tool.

Students saw practical value.

Helped save time 88%
Helped stay on track with dissertation writing 63%

What students told us

Thematic analysis of open-ended responses produced three themes.

Writing habits
It has been wonderful for helping teach me how to more properly structure sentences.Student participant

Students described learning grammar and sentence patterns, not only fixing errors.

Revision strategies
I prefer to get through my writing process first, then check what Grammarly suggests.Student participant

Most turned the tool on late, as a final review step, which sped up proofreading.

Writing confidence
It helped me move through hurdles in sending drafts to my advisor by giving me confidence that the message was clear.Student participant

Students felt more confident that their drafts were clear and polished.

Students also raised a caution: synonym suggestions were sometimes wrong or did not match the intended tone.

Recommendation

The evidence supports recommending Grammarly, or a comparable tool, for broader adoption among graduate students. Pair it with guidance on when in the writing process to use it, and review synonym suggestions critically.

Limits

  • This is a pilot with 13 participants in the statistical analysis, so power is low and results are directional.
  • There was no comparison group, so we cannot attribute the confidence gain to Grammarly alone.
  • Next step: replicate with a larger sample and test how, not just how often, students use the tool.

Graduate writing retreat program evaluation

A pre/post evaluation of the February 2026 writing retreat for graduate students at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, held in person at the Rasmuson Library and online. The findings were written up as a report for the program team.

RoleEvaluation researcher
SettingUAF Graduate School writing support
MethodsPre and post surveys, open-ended questions, thematic analysis
Participants47 registered, 37 attended; 31 pre-survey and 25 post-survey responses

The problem

The Graduate School runs writing retreats to help students make progress on theses, dissertations, and manuscripts. The team needed to know whether the retreat worked, which parts mattered most to students, and what to change next time.

Approach

The retreat ran for three days, with a goal-setting session, seven seminars, daily peer review, and evening reflection sessions. I surveyed attendees before the retreat about their goals, writing confidence, and challenges. After it, I asked about progress, value, and suggestions, using rating questions and open-ended questions. Pre-survey respondents were 58% PhD and 42% Master's students, from seven colleges.

What students brought with them

  • Concrete goals. Most wanted to draft sections of a thesis, dissertation, proposal, or manuscript.
  • Real barriers. 54.9% said they experienced writer's block often or very frequently. Their open-ended answers centered on procrastination, not knowing where to start, and finding a distraction-free place to work.
  • A gap in goal setting. Students rated their understanding of SMART goals at 2.71 out of 5, lower than the 3.2 reported at the previous retreat. This pointed to a need for more instruction on goal setting.

What we found

Students reported progress and value.

Reported a substantial increase in progress over 90%
Rated the retreat very or extremely useful 84%

Every respondent found the retreat useful to some degree. Writing confidence also moved up:

Writing confidenceBefore (31 responses)After (25 responses)
Moderately confident41.9%52%
Very confident25.8%40%

The before and after groups had different numbers of respondents and the surveys were not matched, so this is a descriptive comparison, not a statistical test.

What students told us

Thematic analysis of the open-ended answers produced four themes.

Structured time to write
I liked the 2 hour chunks, because it felt like enough time to really get into a task, but it wasn't so much that I would lose focus.Student participant
Place and practical support
Having food available removed the obstacle of going to purchase food or prepare food at home, so I had more time to focus on writing.Student participant
Seminars and accountability
The accountability session was super helpful. I liked that we checked in with each other and shared our experiences.Student participant
Peer community
I learned that I am not the only one who faces the writer's block, and everyone has their own way to keep themselves motivated.Student participant

The strongest theme was relief at learning that other students struggle with writing too. For students in small departments, the retreat also reduced isolation.

Problems students flagged

  • Some did not know how to book a session with a writing assistant, and one said that barrier kept them from signing up.
  • No dietary or allergy survey went out before the retreat.
  • The main room was crowded, and parking directions were unclear.
  • Requested topics: co-author management, writing for proposals versus grants versus manuscripts, and LaTeX.

Recommendations

Keep the three-day format with protected writing blocks, seminars, and daily accountability check-ins. Strengthen the goal-setting instruction. Make it clear how to book writing support during the retreat. Collect dietary needs at registration, use a larger room, and send campus parking directions in advance. Add seminars on co-author management and on different kinds of academic writing.

Limits

  • Only 25 of 37 attendees answered the post survey, and the before and after samples were not matched.
  • Progress and usefulness are self-reported, and there was no comparison group.

Engineering leadership interview study

An interview study with 13 engineering leaders on how they define leadership based on their lived experience as leaders and which skills matter for leadership success. It grew out of my doctoral research at Utah State University and was published in the European Journal of Engineering Education in 2026.

RoleLead author: conceptualized the study, collected and analyzed the data, wrote the first draft
TeamSix authors, Utah State University
MethodsSemi-structured interviews, interpretative phenomenological analysis, MAXQDA
Participants13 engineering leaders: 6 from ABET, 7 from industry

The problem

Engineering schools are expected to teach leadership, but the field has no agreed definition of it or list of skills to teach. Most existing definitions were borrowed from management and psychology, not built from what engineers say about their own work. Programs end up teaching different things with no shared way to judge whether it works.

Approach

I asked people who live the work: engineers with at least five years of experience and at least two years in a leadership role. Six held leadership positions at ABET, the body that accredits engineering programs, and seven led teams in industry. I interviewed them one on one over Zoom for 45 to 60 minutes with open-ended questions that let me follow each person's story. The study had IRB approval, and the transcripts were de-identified. I coded the transcripts in MAXQDA through repeated reading, noting, theme building, and comparison across all 13 people.

What we found

People explained leadership through stories. Only 5 of the 13 gave a direct definition. The other 8 used examples from their careers. Many hesitated when asked how engineering leadership differs from leadership in general. When I probed further, nearly all pointed to technical expertise as the difference.

ABET and industry leaders agreed. Their definitions did not conflict. We combined them into one proposed definition: engineering leadership joins technical knowledge with interpersonal skills to guide teams toward shared goals, with a commitment to ethics, trust, and collaborative problem-solving.

Leaders named 18 skills. All 13 said technical expertise is essential, and they paired it with skills such as communication, listening, empathy, teamwork, explaining the reasons behind decisions, and humility. I counted how often each skill came up for each group, then sorted the skills into a three-level taxonomy:

Level 1: measurable

Technical expertise, problem-solving, decision-making, organization and time management. These can be tested.

Level 2: observable

Communication, teamwork, listening, empathy, explaining the why, flexibility. These show up in how people interact and can be assessed through feedback or interviews.

Level 3: personal

Humility, fearless exploration, lifelong learning, strategic vision, ethics, leadership identity. These can be assessed through reflection.

Leadership grows in three stages

Self-directed

Entry-level engineers lead their own work with little supervision.

Team

Project managers lead people they have no formal authority over.

Managerial

Formal organizational leadership, which many engineers choose not to pursue.

Every engineer passes through at least the first two stages, so all engineers are leaders, but not all are managerial leaders. Many participants also said they had no leadership training and learned by making mistakes.

Recommendations

Engineering programs should teach a clear definition of engineering leadership, help students see themselves as leaders from graduation, and give them practice in interpersonal skills such as communication, listening, and teamwork. The paper suggests leadership labs with role-play and industry partners for this.

Limits

  • All industry participants and all companies were in Utah, and all ABET participants were in academia.
  • Participation was voluntary, which may limit the range of genders, ethnicities, sectors, and experience.
  • The taxonomy is an early proposal that still needs testing.
